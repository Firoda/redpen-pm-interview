Assignment 1: Personas and Problem Hypotheses
Software Product Management · PGP Co'27 Term 5 · Individual Assignment
Submitted by: Aditya Firoda · Date: 8 November 2026

Product scenario
Product: an interview preparation tool that helps working professionals get ready for job interviews
when they move to a new company or a new function.
Research question: how do working professionals prepare for interviews today, and where does that
preparation break down?
Method: five short, semi-structured interviews (about 5–10 minutes each) with mid-career
professionals in finance, product, engineering, consulting and strategy. Four of the five have also
interviewed candidates, so they spoke from both sides of the table. Audio, verbatim transcripts and
coded responses are in the Appendix.
What I found: the hardest part of preparing is not effort but direction. People do not know what a
specific role will test, they cannot get trustworthy feedback while working full-time, and the volume
of scattered material makes many give up.

Problem Hypothesis 1
The mid-career professionals switching to a new function, company or industry are most frustrated
about working out what a specific role will test and what to prepare because preparation material is
generic and scattered, and the real round formats and question types stay a “black box” until they
are in the interview.
Evidence: Dhanush missed a core PM question type; Vinay calls startup processes a black box;
Jinendra, as an interviewer, sees candidates fail because they never research the specific role.
How to test it: Ask 10 more switchers to list the rounds and question types they expect for a target
job, then compare with the real process. Hypothesis holds if most miss at least one round type.
Problem Hypothesis 2
The working professionals preparing on their own while employed full-time are most frustrated
about getting trustworthy feedback on their answers and knowing when they are ready because they
have no practice partners or people from the target role, so they rely on non-expert colleagues or
generic AI and never get a clear readiness signal.
Evidence: Pritish had no case partners and relied on ChatGPT; Dhanush's feedback came from non-
product seniors; Manraj is “not very confident” for senior rounds.
How to test it: Run AI mock interviews with feedback for 10 users, then compare their self-rated
readiness with an expert's rating. A large gap supports the hypothesis.

Problem Hypothesis 3
The long-tenure professionals (4+ years at one company, promoted internally) are most frustrated
about preparing consistently for external interviews alongside their job because internal moves never
tested their interview skills, and the volume of scattered material feels overwhelming, so they start
preparing and then drop it.
Evidence: Manraj, Jinendra and Dhanush all moved internally on track record; Manraj started and
dropped prep because it felt overwhelming.
How to test it: Survey professionals who tried to switch: how many started preparing and stopped,
and why. Hypothesis holds if overwhelm or lack of structure is a top-two reason.

Problem Hypothesis 4
The breadth-first generalists with non-linear careers are most frustrated about presenting their
experience as the best fit for a specialised role because nothing carries over between job hunts (no
story bank) and every startup process tests a different skill set.
Evidence: Vinay finds his broad profile hard to explain and keeps no stories or past questions;
Jinendra's one-line advice is “know what you did and position yourself as the best fit”.
How to test it: Offer a story-bank prototype to 5 generalists; measure whether they reuse it across
two applications.
Priority: Hypotheses 1 and 2 are the strongest. Each was raised unprompted by at least three
interviewees, and they point to the product's core: decoding what a role tests, then giving realistic
practice with feedback. Hypotheses 3 and 4 shape engagement features (structured plans, story
bank).

A.1 Personas
A.1.1 How I selected interview candidates
I looked for people likely to face an important job interview in the next one to two years, and picked
them to cover different situations rather than one type of user.
 • Target user: working professionals with roughly 4–8 years of experience, the core market for the
   product.
 • Variety of functions: finance, product management, software engineering, consulting and
   strategy.
 • Variety of company types: a telecom company, an investment bank, a global tech company, a
   boutique consulting firm and startups.
 • Variety of career paths: internal movers (promoted or moved within one company) and external
   switchers (across companies, functions or industries).
 • Both sides of the table: four of the five have interviewed candidates, so they could describe
   what interviewers look for as well as how candidates prepare.
 • One degree of separation: all were reached through my ISB and professional network. None is a
   close friend or family member.
 • Excluded: students. I replaced a planned student interview with a senior software engineer to
   keep the focus on working professionals.
